# Industry cases: making wheels bigger (successes and failures)

Compiled 2026-10-07 for Part C of the paper (industry precedent for a larger front wheel).
Every source in the bibliography was opened on 2026-10-07 and the quoted facts were found
there, except where marked **[unchecked]**. Company figures are company claims and are not
independently audited unless stated.

A caution for the paper: none of these cases isolates wheel size. Every redesign also
changed other things (suspension, frame, battery, wheelbase), so they show what companies
*did* and what happened next, not that the wheel caused the outcome.

## Cases in detail

### 1. Voi: Voiager 5 and Voiager 7 (success, closest precedent)
- Voiager 5 (March 2022): "a larger front wheel with extra tyre tread depth and increased
  shock absorbers. The ride height of the machine has also been raised so that it can cope
  better over potholes." [Attwood 2022]
- Voiager 7 (April 2024): "A 12-inch front tyre, a size increase from its predecessors",
  for "a safer and more comfortable ride over bumpy road surfaces from cobblestones to
  potholes". Expected lifespan eight years; 36 kg. Rear size not stated. [Startups Magazine 2024]
- Commercial result: 2024 was Voi's first profitable year (adjusted EBITDA EUR 17.2 m,
  adjusted EBIT about EUR 0.1 m; vehicle margin 57% vs 49% in 2023). The CEO credited
  longer vehicle life (about eight years) and predictive maintenance, **not** wheel size.
  [Bellan 2025]
- Use in the paper: Voi itself has twice enlarged the front wheel and named potholes as
  the reason. This is the strongest precedent and comes from the operator in the study.
  Do not claim the wheel made Voi profitable.

### 2. Lime Gen4 (success)
- Announced November 2020 with "an enhanced suspension and larger wheels"; built partly by
  former Uber Jump engineers. Announced with Lime's first quarter of positive operating and
  free cash flow (Q3 2020). [Korosec 2020]
- Lime reports that after rollout, average ratings rose from 3.5 to 4.1 stars and average
  trips from 8 to 11 minutes (35,000 scooters, 30+ cities). [Lime 2021] **[company blog
  returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch; text seen in search snippets only, open it in a
  browser before citing]**
- Wheel diameter not given in these sources.

### 3. Bird Three (mixed: better vehicle, failed company)
- Launched May 2021: 10 in self-sealing pneumatic tyres front and rear, "elongated
  wheelbase for improved stability". [Toll 2021; Levy n.d.; Maxwell 2021]
- Bird claimed a "nearly 400 percent drop in damages and a 185 percent increase in rides
  taken per vehicle" in early test markets. [Maxwell 2021] (A "400% drop" is not
  arithmetically meaningful; quote it as Bird's wording.)
- Bird Global filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on 20 December 2023, after being delisted
  for overstating revenue. [Bloomberg Law 2023; TechCrunch 2023]
- Lesson: sturdier vehicles with bigger tyres improve vehicle economics but do not by
  themselves save a business with other problems.

### 4. First-generation shared scooters (failure that drove the change)
- Louisville data, Aug-Dec 2018: average scooter lifespan 28 days, median 23. Early
  fleets were "rebranded Xiaomi devices intended for use by a single owner". Estimated
  loss of USD 285-295 per scooter. [Griswold 2019]
- Operators answered with purpose-built scooters (Bird with Okai, Lime Gen3 with wider
  base and wheels); the later generations in cases 1-3 have bigger tyres. The Xiaomi M365
  used 8.5 in tyres [unchecked: confirm from Xiaomi spec].

### 5. Mullet mountain bikes (success; front-only enlargement)
- UCI relaxed the rule on mixed wheel diameters for 2019; Specialized's downhill team
  raced a 29 in front / 27.5 in rear Demo. The 2019 Demo was described as the most
  successful downhill bike of that season and Loïc Bruni became world champion on it.
  [Evans 2020; MBR 2019]
- Specialized then sold a dedicated mullet Demo (2020, 2021); Santa Cruz, Canyon and
  others now sell mullet bikes. [Evans 2020; Canyon n.d.]
- Rationale stated: the "rollover, bump-eating capabilities of the 29er" at the front with
  the handling of the smaller rear. [Evans 2020]
- Use in the paper: the closest commercial parallel to the "larger front wheel only"
  hypothesis. Different vehicle (bicycle, rider seated or standing on pedals, suspension
  both ends), so analogy only.

### 6. 29er mountain bikes (slow success)
- Large-wheel off-road bikes date to Geoff Apps (early 1980s); the industry stayed on 26 in
  partly because big tyres were scarce. Gary Fisher (Trek) launched the Two Niner in 2001
  but the line "never sold well" until the single-speed Rig (2004). [Wikipedia 29er]
- By 2016 Nino Schurter won the Olympic XC title on a 29er; in 2020 WTB said about 90% of
  its sales were 29 in. [Brújula Bike n.d.; Miller 2020]
- Lesson: a geometrically better wheel can take a decade or more to sell; supply chains
  (tyres, forks, frames) matter as much as performance.

### 7. 27.5+ "plus" tyres (failure)
- Heavily promoted 2015-2016; by 2020 "companies pushing the idea gave up and plus bikes
  have disappeared from the catalogs". Salsa's Timberjack moved from ~75% 27.5+ (2019) to
  a 50/50 forecast (2020). [Miller 2020; MTB-Mag n.d.]
- Lesson: bigger is not automatically better; the plus format lost on weight, tyre
  squirm and the rise of 29ers [the reasons are trade-press opinion].

### 8. Kickbike (niche success; front-only enlargement)
- Finnish kick scooter (Kickbike Worldwide, founded 1994 by Hannu Vierikko) with a
  bicycle-size front wheel and a much smaller rear wheel; used in racing and dog scootering.
  [Wikipedia Kickbike] Weak source; find a company or museum source if cited.

### 9. Penny-farthing (failure on safety)
- The front wheel was made large for speed (direct drive) and a smoother ride on rough
  roads; the rider sat almost over the front axle, so a wheel hitting a rut or stone
  pitched the rider over the front ("a header"), sometimes fatally. Production ended by
  1893 after chain-drive "safety bicycles" with equal wheels (Starley's Rover, 1885) and
  pneumatic tyres (Dunlop, 1888). [Wikipedia Penny-farthing; Wikipedia Safety bicycle;
  Herlihy 2004 **[unchecked]**]
- Use in the paper: a large front wheel is not safe in itself; the failure came from rider
  position over the front axle and pitch-over, the same mode as the simulated falls. Our
  proposal changes wheel size without raising the rider.

### 10. Cars: bigger alloys, lower-profile tyres (profitable, but worse on potholes)
- Insurer data (Intelligent Motoring): average alloy-wheel claim values up 18% in five
  years; brands with larger wheels (BMW, Mercedes, Audi) claim most; "larger wheels and
  lower-profile tyres ... provide less cushioning against potholes". [Bodyshop 2026]
- Lesson for the paper: what helps on potholes is more tyre and wheel radius, not a
  bigger rim with a thinner tyre. Specify overall wheel diameter and tyre volume.
- That car makers use large wheels as a profitable trim upgrade is widely stated but no
  source was checked here **[unchecked]**.

## What this means for the paper (Part C)
- The front-only enlargement has two real commercial precedents (Voi itself; mullet MTBs)
  and one older one (kickbike). Voi named potholes as its reason.
- Operators' profits improved alongside sturdier, larger-wheeled vehicles (Lime, Voi), but
  the sources credit durability and operations, not wheels; say "accompanied", not "caused".
- Failures: bigger hardware did not save Bird; plus tyres show that a wheel change the
  market does not want dies fast; penny-farthing shows pitch-over risk depends on rider
  position as well as wheel size; cars show rim size without tyre volume makes potholes
  worse.
