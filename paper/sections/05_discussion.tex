\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Main findings}

On three rides totalling 6.08~km, a handlebar-mounted phone recorded 100 vertical impacts of
1~g or more on Voiager 5 scooters, about one every 61~m, and a third of them reached 1.5~g
(Section~\ref{summary-of-part-a}). Ten places produced impacts on two or more separate rides,
two of them on all three, and most of these had no FixMyStreet report in the preceding year.
The recording method itself held up: the motion sensors sampled at a steady rate with no
gaps, and the walk at the end of one recording was separated from riding to within the
minute-level precision of the available references (Appendix~B).

In the model, a larger front wheel reduced falls. With the Voiager 5's front suspension, the
current 10/10 configuration fell in 9 of 81 runs, a 12~in front wheel in 6, and a 14~in front
wheel in 1 (Table~\ref{tab:7}). The hypothesis survived the test that could have refuted it:
enlarging the front wheel alone (12/10) gave the same number of falls and the same falling
depths as enlarging both wheels (12/12) in every case of the main sweep, with and without
suspension, and in 8 of the 9 settings of the sensitivity analysis with suspension (the
exception, more fork damping, differed by one 10~mm step; Section~\ref{sensitivity}). The mechanism in the model is that the front-edge impact lifts
the rear of the scooter, so that the rear wheel largely skips the pothole. All modelled
pothole falls were forward pitch-overs about the front wheel, the mechanism also reported by
\textcite{posirisuk2022}. With sloped pothole edges, a larger front wheel still
reduced falls at every edge width, and 12/10 stayed within one fall of 12/12
(Table~\ref{tab:8}).

Suspension mattered as much as wheel size. Without it, falls were more frequent for every
configuration (15, 14, 14 and 11 of 81 runs), and the suspended model's handlebar
accelerations matched the measured range on short potholes where the rigid model's did not
(Section~\ref{consistency-with-the-measured-rides}). This agrees with the recommendation of
\textcite{paudel2025} that small-wheeled vehicles should have both larger wheels and front
suspension.

\subsection{What the results do not show}

The results describe exposure on these rides and the behaviour of a model; they do not
measure risk. The measurements come from one rider, one phone and short routes in one city,
and the 1.0~g threshold, the severity bands and the wobble ratio are analyst choices rather
than established criteria. No impact was linked to a fall or near-fall, and nothing here
supports a statement about the safety of Voi scooters, about injury risk, or about other
riders.

The model is planar: it has no steering, roll or sideways fall, so it cannot represent the
loss of steering control that a front-wheel impact may also cause. Its mass, inertias,
wheelbase, tyre and fork properties are assumptions, and the rider is a single body on a
fixed spring. Its absolute falling depths (60--100~mm, depending on suspension, speed and
tyre stiffness) are not real-world thresholds and should not be compared directly with the
6~cm of \textcite{posirisuk2022}, whose model differs in rider, scooter and contact
representation. The comparison between wheel configurations under the same assumptions is
the result; the consistency check against the rides supports the suspended model's jolt
sizes on short potholes only and is not a validation, because the depths of the potholes on
the route were not measured.

The front-only hypothesis was tested on potholes. In an exploratory extension to raised,
sharp-edged bumps (Appendix~\ref{app:bumps}), larger front wheels again reduced falls in the
rigid model, but with the front spring the few modelled bump falls followed the strike of the
rigid rear wheel. Whether a front-only rule covers every kind of obstacle is therefore an open
question. Finally, the wheel sizes of the Voiager 5 are reported, not measured, and no source
separates front and rear; if its front wheel were larger than the reported 10~in, the
baseline of the model would shift, although the comparison between configurations would not.

\subsection{Implications for trial vehicle rules}

The national trial rules set no minimum wheel size, while London requires a front wheel of at
least 11~in \autocite{dft2024guidance,tfl2022tender}. A scooter with a reported 10~in front
wheel, below London's minimum, remains in service outside London, as in Oxford, and was
deployed in a new trial area in 2025 \autocite{essex2025voi}. Within the limits above, the
model suggests that a larger front wheel delays pothole falls and that, for potholes, a
front-wheel minimum captures the benefit of larger wheels without requiring both wheels to be
enlarged. Because only trial rental scooters are legal on public roads, a small number of
actors can act on this:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The Department for Transport} could add a front-wheel minimum, such as London's,
  to the national trial vehicle requirements, and consider whether its 5~cm stability test is
  deep enough to separate wheel sizes: in the model, no configuration fell in a 5~cm deep
  pothole, so a test at that depth would not have shown the differences in falls between them.
\item \textbf{Operators}, when renewing fleets outside London, could prioritise models with a
  larger front wheel and front suspension. Voi's own newer models already have larger front
  wheels \autocite{roadcc2022voiager5,startups2024voiager7}.
\item \textbf{Local authorities hosting trials}, such as Oxfordshire County Council, could ask for
  London-equivalent vehicle standards when awarding or extending trial contracts, and could use
  repeated impacts at the same places as one input to road inspection.
\item \textbf{Research groups with test facilities} could test front-wheel size on instrumented
  scooters over pothole-shaped obstacles; this is the step needed to confirm or refute the model.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future work}

The most useful next steps are: measuring the front and rear tyre sizes of the Voiager 5
directly; recording more rides, riders and phones with the same method, under an ethics
approval if other riders are involved; measuring the depth of the defects at repeatedly hit
spots so that the model can be validated rather than checked for consistency; extending the
model to three dimensions with steering; and physical tests of the kind described above.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A smartphone on the handlebar of a rental e-scooter recorded frequent, repeated vertical
impacts on ordinary Oxford roads, often at places with no recent public report. A dynamics
model of the Voiager 5 suggests that a larger front wheel reduces pothole falls and that
enlarging the front wheel alone gives the same benefit as enlarging both, a hypothesis the
model could have refuted but did not. These are results from three rides and an unvalidated
model, not proof that current scooters are unsafe or that larger wheels prevent falls. They
are, however, consistent with London's existing front-wheel rule and with the direction
operators have already taken, and they justify both extending a front-wheel minimum to
trials outside London and testing it physically.
