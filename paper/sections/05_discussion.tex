\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Main findings}

On three rides totalling 6.08~km, a handlebar-mounted phone recorded 100 vertical impacts of
1~g or more on Voiager 5 scooters, about one every 61~m, and a third of them reached 1.5~g
(Section~\ref{summary-of-part-a}). Ten places produced impacts on two or more separate rides,
two of them on all three, and most of these had no FixMyStreet report in the preceding year.
The recording method itself held up: the motion sensors sampled at a steady rate with no
gaps, and the walk at the end of one recording was separated from riding to within the
minute-level precision of the available references (Appendix~B).

The Voiager 5 that was ridden has, from its tyre markings, an 11.1~in front wheel and a
10.1~in rear wheel (Figure~\ref{fig:tyres}). In the model with its front suspension, this
11/10 configuration fell in 9 of 81 runs, the same number and at the same depths as a
10/10 reference, while a 12~in front wheel fell in 6 and a 14~in front wheel in 1
(Table~\ref{tab:7}). In this model, then, the benefit of a larger front wheel began above the
Voiager 5's current size. The difference between 10 and 11~in may be smaller than the
model's 10~mm depth resolution can show, so ``no benefit'' should be read as ``no benefit
detected''.

The hypothesis survived the test that could have refuted it: enlarging the front wheel
alone (12/10) gave the same number of falls and the same falling depths as enlarging both
wheels (12/12) in every case of the main sweep, with and without suspension, and in 8 of
the 9 settings of the sensitivity analysis with suspension (the exception, more fork
damping, differed by one 10~mm step; Section~\ref{sensitivity}). The mechanism in the model
is that the front-edge impact lifts the rear of the scooter, so that the rear wheel largely
skips the pothole. All but one of the modelled pothole falls were forward pitch-overs
started by the front-wheel impact, the mechanism also reported by \textcite{posirisuk2022};
the exception, one 11/10 run, was credited to the rear wheel only because the rear dipped
5.8~mm, just over the 5~mm attribution rule. With sloped pothole edges, 12 and 14~in front
wheels fell no more often than the Voiager 5 at any edge width, and 12/10 stayed within one
fall of 12/12 (Table~\ref{tab:8}).

Suspension also mattered. Without it, falls were more frequent for every configuration (15,
15, 14, 14 and 11 of 81 runs), and the suspended model's handlebar accelerations matched the
measured range on short potholes where the rigid model's did not
(Section~\ref{consistency-with-the-measured-rides}). This agrees with the recommendation of
\textcite{paudel2025} that small-wheeled vehicles should have both larger wheels and front
suspension.

\subsection{What the results do not show}

The results describe exposure on these rides and the behaviour of a model; they do not
measure risk. The measurements come from one rider, one phone and short routes in one city,
and the 1.0~g threshold, the severity bands and the wobble ratio are analyst choices rather
than established criteria. No impact was linked to a fall or near-fall, and nothing here
supports a statement about the safety of Voi scooters, about injury risk, or about other
riders.

The model is planar: it has no steering, roll or sideways fall, so it cannot represent the
loss of steering control that a front-wheel impact may also cause. Its mass, inertias,
wheelbase, tyre and fork properties are assumptions, and the rider is a single body on a
fixed spring. Its absolute falling depths (60--100~mm, depending on suspension, speed and
tyre stiffness) are not real-world thresholds and should not be compared directly with the
6~cm of \textcite{posirisuk2022}, whose model differs in rider, scooter and contact
representation. The comparison between wheel configurations under the same assumptions is
the result; the consistency check against the rides supports the suspended model's jolt
sizes on short potholes only and is not a validation, because the depths of the potholes on
the route were not measured.

The front-only hypothesis was tested on potholes. In an exploratory extension to raised,
sharp-edged bumps (Appendix~\ref{app:bumps}), larger front wheels again reduced falls in the
rigid model, but with the front spring the few modelled bump falls followed the strike of the
rigid rear wheel. Whether a front-only rule covers every kind of obstacle is therefore an open
question. Finally, the wheel sizes come from the sidewall markings of a Voiager 5, the
model ridden, and are nominal sizes of new tyres; worn tyres under load are slightly
smaller.

\subsection{Implications for trial vehicle rules}

The national trial guidance and the legal definition of a trial e-scooter set no minimum
wheel size, while London requires a front wheel of at least 11~in
\autocite{dft2024guidance,si2020_663,tfl2022tender}. The Voiager 5 in service in Oxford meets
London's minimum, by about 2.5~mm. Outside London, the national rules do not require even
that; whether individual trial areas set their own wheel-size conditions in their
contracts, as London does, was not checked. Within the
limits above, the model suggests two things: that a larger front wheel delays pothole falls,
and that a front wheel at London's 11~in minimum did not fall less often than a 10~in one,
whereas 12 and 14~in front wheels did. For potholes, a front-wheel minimum also captured the
benefit of larger wheels without requiring both wheels to be enlarged. Because only trial
rental scooters are legal on public roads, a small number of actors can act on this:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The Department for Transport} could add a front-wheel minimum to the national
  trial vehicle requirements, at least London's, and consider whether a larger one (12~in
  or more, with front suspension) would be justified once physically tested. It could also
  consider whether its 5~cm stability test is deep enough to separate wheel sizes: in the
  model, no configuration fell in a 5~cm deep pothole, so a test at that depth would not
  have shown the differences in falls between them.
\item \textbf{Transport for London} could review whether its 11~in front-wheel minimum is
  sufficient, since in this model a front wheel at that size fell as often as a 10~in one.
\item \textbf{Operators}, when renewing fleets, could prioritise models with a front wheel
  larger than 11~in and with front suspension.
\item \textbf{Local authorities hosting trials}, such as Oxfordshire County Council, could ask for
  at least London-equivalent vehicle standards when awarding or extending trial contracts,
  and could use repeated impacts at the same places as one input to road inspection.
\item \textbf{Research groups with test facilities} could test front-wheel size on instrumented
  scooters over pothole-shaped obstacles; this is the step needed to confirm or refute the model.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future work}

The most useful next steps are: recording more rides, riders and phones with the same method, under an ethics approval if
other riders are involved; measuring the depth of the defects at repeatedly hit spots so that
the model can be validated rather than checked for consistency; refining the model's depth
resolution around the 10--12~in front sizes; extending the model to three dimensions with
steering; and physical tests of the kind described above.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A smartphone on the handlebar of a rental e-scooter recorded frequent, repeated vertical
impacts on ordinary Oxford roads, often at places with no recent public report. The
Voiager 5 ridden has an 11~in front wheel, at London's minimum. A dynamics model of it
suggests that this front wheel falls in potholes about as often as a 10~in one, that 12 and
14~in front wheels reduce pothole falls, and that enlarging the front wheel alone gives the
same benefit as enlarging both, a hypothesis the model could have refuted but did not. These
are results from three rides and an unvalidated model, not proof that current scooters are
unsafe or that larger wheels prevent falls. They do justify extending a front-wheel
minimum to trials outside London, asking whether London's 11~in minimum is high enough, and
testing larger front wheels physically.
